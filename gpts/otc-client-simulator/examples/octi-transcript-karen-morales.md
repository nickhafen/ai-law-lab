## Evaluation

A standard intake interview with Karen about HireTech, the AI resume screening tool that HR uses. The student asked how the tool fits into hiring, then about the vendor's bias audit, the careers-page disclosure and accommodations, which state laws might apply, candidate complaints, and who owns the vendor relationship. They ended with a clear list of follow-up documents.

Against the scenario's open risk areas:

- **The vendor hasn't shared its bias metrics:** surfaced. Karen confirmed HireTech ran its own audit and that she hasn't reviewed the underlying calculations or raw data.
- **Minimal disclosure to applicants:** surfaced. The careers page says only "Some roles use automated screening," and applicants have no opt-out or alternative process.
- **NYC Local Law 144 compliance still under review:** surfaced and flagged as a priority.

### Takeaways

- **All three core issues surfaced.** Opening with "walk me through what the tool actually does" before getting to Karen's documents set up the rest of the interview well.
- **The student stated legal conclusions during intake.** Saying what Local Law 144 requires and calling it "a real gap" is counseling rather than fact-finding. It's worth asking whether that belongs in a first meeting.
- **"95% parity" went unexamined.** The student never asked what the figure measures or how it was calculated. That question is the intended way to break through Karen's "we have a document for that" habit.
- **The persona was easier than written.** Karen is designed to answer questions by pointing to documents until she's walked through them. Here she was candid about her gaps almost immediately, so the shift the scenario builds toward never really happened.

**Karen Morales:** Hi, I'm Karen Morales, VP of People & Culture. Thanks for meeting with me. We've been using HireTech's screening tool since January, and I want to make sure everything is above board. I actually put together a few documents that might be helpful—we have the vendor's bias report, our internal policy, and the disclosure language on our careers page. I think we've been pretty thorough, but I heard there's some new state legislation and I want to make sure we're covered.

Where would you like to start?

**Student:** Thanks, Karen. It's great that you've already pulled together the bias report, internal policy, and careers-page disclosure — I'll want copies of all three. Before we get into those documents, can you walk me through what the HireTech tool actually does in your hiring process — what decisions it makes or influences, and which roles or locations it's used for?

**Karen Morales:** Sure. HireTech sits at the front end of our recruiting process: applicants submit a resume, cover letter, and responses to three situational-judgment questions, and the tool scores and ranks them for recruiter review. It doesn't make the final hiring decision, but its ranking influences which candidates recruiters look at first, and if a recruiter overrides that ranking, they're supposed to document the reason in our ATS.

We use it only for certain roles that get a higher volume of applicants, not every opening. I don't have the exact role-and-location list in front of me, though—our recruiting operations team maintains that, and I'd want to get you the current list rather than guess.

**Student:** That's helpful — please do get me that role-and-location list. On the bias report you mentioned: who conducted it, how recently, and does it break down results by race, sex, and other protected categories? And separately, what exactly does the careers-page disclosure tell applicants — does it say an AI tool is used, and do applicants have any way to request an alternative process or accommodation?

**Karen Morales:** The bias report was conducted by HireTech itself in December 2024. The headline finding they gave us was "95% parity" across gender and ethnicity, and the report breaks out those categories at a high level, but I haven't reviewed the underlying calculations or raw test data. I'd need to check whether it covers other protected categories like age or disability, because I don't want to overstate what's in it.

Our careers page currently says, "Some roles use automated screening." It doesn't explain how the tool works or what applicant data is used, and as far as I know it doesn't offer a general opt-out or alternative screening process. For accommodations, we have our normal recruiting accommodation process, but I'd need to confirm whether we specifically connect that process to HireTech in the applicant-facing materials.

**Student:** That's useful, and flags a couple of gaps I'll want to note — a vendor self-conducted audit versus an independent one can matter a lot depending on which law applies. You mentioned you'd heard about new state legislation — do you know which state or law prompted that concern, and what specifically made you think it might affect HireTech? Also, does the company operate in New York City or Illinois, where there are specific automated employment decision tool laws already on the books?

**Karen Morales:** The one that came up internally was New York City's law on automated employment decision tools. Legal flagged it because HireTech is used in screening, but I don't know the requirements well enough to tell you whether our current process satisfies them. Our team has treated that as still under review rather than something I can say is closed out.

We do recruit in Illinois, and I believe we've had New York-area applicants as well, but I'd want to confirm whether we've actually used HireTech for positions located in New York City specifically. That should be part of the role-and-location list I'm getting you.

**Student:** That's an important open item — NYC Local Law 144 requires an independent bias audit and specific notice to candidates, so if HireTech has been used for any NYC-based roles since January, that's a real gap we need to close quickly. Let me ask a couple more: has any rejected candidate ever complained about the screening process or requested to know why they were passed over? And who internally owns the vendor relationship and contract with HireTech — would they have documentation on what data was shared with HireTech to build or validate the model?

**Karen Morales:** I'm not aware of any formal complaint from a rejected candidate specifically about HireTech or a request for an explanation of the tool's ranking, but I wouldn't want to say there have been none without checking with recruiting operations. They would have the candidate communications and any escalation records.

On the vendor side, People & Culture owns the business relationship, with our recruiting operations team handling the day-to-day contact. Legal reviewed the contract, and HireTech would have the better documentation on how the model was trained or validated; what I know is that they told us it was trained on "industry standard" hiring data and was not customized using OTC's historical hires. I have the contract summary, but I haven't personally dug into what audit rights or data-access rights we negotiated.

**Student:** This gives me a good starting picture — thank you. To summarize what I'll need from you: the role-and-location list showing where HireTech has been used since January, the full bias report and contract with HireTech (including any audit or data-access rights), and confirmation from recruiting operations on any candidate complaints or requests for explanation. I'll also want to flag the NYC Local Law 144 gap to you and Legal as a priority item before we go further. Let's regroup once you have those.

**Karen Morales:** Absolutely. I'll pull the role-and-location history, get you the full HireTech bias report and contract, and have recruiting operations check for any candidate complaints or explanation requests. I'll also make sure Legal knows you've identified the New York City issue as a priority for review before we expand our use of the tool.

That gives me a clear list to work from.
